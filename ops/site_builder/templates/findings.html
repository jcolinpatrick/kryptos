Three things we did find

No single classical cipher decrypts K4. But the analysis wasn't fruitless. Three notable patterns emerged from the data. The first two were confirmed independently; the third was discovered post-hoc and requires further validation. Together they constrain how K4 was constructed, even though the cipher itself remains unbroken.

Why we trust these findings

Each finding is based on reproducible analysis, not speculation. Findings 1 and 2 were confirmed by independent evidence from multiple directions:

  • Independent replication: Richard Bean found the same 5-wide Polybius structure in 2021 (HistoCrypt 2021) by analyzing the keystream. We found it independently by analyzing the null characters. Two researchers, different data, same result.
  • Exhaustive verification: We tested all 232 million possible placements of the remaining null positions. The 17 consensus positions appear in every configuration that scores above random noise.
  • Cross-layer convergence: The Beaufort keystream at the first 8 BERLINCLOCK positions (63–70) is 7/8 palette letters (p=0.0006 unconditional). Overall, 13/24 keystream values at all crib positions are palette letters (p=0.0043). Sub-patterns within this enrichment (boundary sharpness, letter repeats, spatial clustering) are conditional on the base rate and do not carry independent significance. The enrichment itself is alphabet-independent (a property of the carved text, not any cipher model).
  • Combined odds of coincidence: p < 10−7 (Fisher’s method on palette restriction + keystream enrichment; confirmed by joint Monte Carlo, 0 hits in 10M trials).

The entire codebase is open source. Every finding includes a reproduction command.

The Null Palette

Only 7 of 26 letters appear at candidate filler positions

p < 10−7
High confidence

The short version

Someone chose exactly 7 letters (B, G, I, K, O, W, and Z) and scattered them through K4 as filler characters. The odds of this restriction happening by chance are about 1 in 33,000. This means K4 probably isn’t 97 characters of real ciphertext. It’s probably 73, with 24 decoys designed to make frequency analysis useless.

1 in 33,000 probability of seeing this palette restriction by chance
Technical detail

K4 has 97 characters carved into the sculpture, but our analysis suggests that only 73 may be real ciphertext. Under this hypothesis, the other 24 are filler characters (called nulls) inserted to disguise the message’s true length. This is consistent with Ed Scheidt’s public statement that he “masked the English language” in K4 so that frequency analysis would not help solvers, and with Sanborn’s legal pad notation “8 lines 73” (auctioned publicly in 2025).

We identified 17 positions where independent analyses converge on the character being a null. Those 17 characters use only 7 different letters: B, G, I, K, O, W, and Z. Drawing 17 characters at random from K4’s letter distribution and getting only 7 distinct letters has a probability of roughly 1 in 33,000 (permutation test, 10M trials). This is very unlikely to be coincidence.

Even more striking: 4 of the 5 W’s in the entire ciphertext are nulls. The single non-null W sits at position 48, exactly halfway between the two known plaintext regions.

What this means, and what it doesn't

This tells us which letters can appear as filler: only {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}, drawn from two columns of a 5-wide keyword-mixed Polybius grid. It does not tell us which specific palette letter fills each null position. We tested 7 models (position formulas, neighbor functions, keystream echo, grid cell mapping, cipher determination, delta constraints) and all failed (best: 6/17). The character assignment requires information not present in the ciphertext, most likely Sanborn’s encoding chart.

The specific keyword is unknown; KRYPTOS is one of ~6,300 English words that produce this column pattern. It does not crack the cipher operating on the remaining 73 real characters.

The Stehle Anomaly

A unique constant-difference pattern at positions 55–63

p ≈ 0.0016
Moderate confidence

The short version

Every 4th character in one region of K4 (positions 55–63) differs from its predecessor by exactly 5 in the alphabet. This pattern appears nowhere else in the ciphertext. After correcting for 712 statistical tests, the odds of this happening by chance are about 1 in 642.

Technical detail

If you look at every 4th character in K4 starting from position 55, something unusual happens: each character is exactly 5 positions later in the alphabet than the one 4 spots before it. This holds for a run of 9 consecutive characters (positions 55–63).

We searched the entire ciphertext for this kind of pattern at every possible spacing and every possible difference value. This is the only one. After correcting for the 712 statistical tests we ran, the probability of seeing this by chance is about 1 in 642 (Bonferroni-corrected: 712 / 456,976).

What creates this pattern

Two of the 9 positions (58 and 59) are consensus null positions. Deeper analysis reveals the anatomy: the cipher layer already produced Δ4=5 at two consecutive non-null pairs (56→60 and 57→61). These are 2 of only 3 such consecutive matches in the entire ciphertext at lag 4. The null values at positions 58 and 59 simply extend this existing pattern, and both forced values (W and I) happen to be palette letters, so the null insertion is compatible.

Position 59 is doubly constrained: D(55)+5 = I and N(63)−5 = I both force the same value from opposite sides. Position 58 is constrained only from the right (B(62)−5 = W). We tested whether this arithmetic forcing is a general null-value generation mechanism: it is not. Only 4 of 17 null positions are constrained this way, and the total count is exactly what random values would produce (MC p=0.59). There is no global constant-difference rule (tested all 1,248 lag×delta combinations, p=0.954 after Bonferroni). The Stehle pattern is a local coincidence (a fortunate collision between cipher output structure and null placement), not a generative mechanism.

KRYPTOS × SEVEN

A dual-keyword lookup table that fits every null position (discovered post-hoc)

35 / 35 correct
Suggestive only (discovered post-hoc)

The short version

A simple mathematical rule using the keywords KRYPTOS and SEVEN correctly classifies all 35 palette-letter positions as null or real (35 out of 35). But this was discovered from the data, not predicted in advance, and cross-validation shows it has zero predictive power.

Technical detail

We discovered a simple rule that perfectly fits all 35 palette-letter positions, classifying each as either null or real ciphertext. The rule uses two keywords: KRYPTOS (7 letters) and SEVEN (5 letters). This was found by searching the data (post-hoc), not predicted in advance.

For any position p in K4, compute two remainders: divide p by 7 (giving a row in the KRYPTOS keyword) and divide p by 5 (giving a column in the SEVEN keyword). This creates a 7×5 lookup table with 35 cells. Each cell is marked Null or Real. The table correctly predicts all 17 consensus null positions and all 18 real palette positions, with zero errors.

The number 35 is not a coincidence: 7 × 5 = 35, and there are exactly 35 positions in K4 where a palette letter ({B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}) appears. The two keyword lengths produce a repeating cycle of exactly the right size.

Why this is interesting (but not proven)

The KRYPTOS keyword was used for K1–K3. SEVEN could connect to FIVE, which appears at the seam of the Kryptos cylinder. The Beaufort cipher with key N transforms {E,H,N,Q,S,T,V} into the null palette, and that source set contains the word SEVEN. If real, this would be an elegant, hand-executable system consistent with 1990 CIA tradecraft.

The N/R cell assignments in the table can be generated by Vigenère(KRYPTOS, CHART) on the standard alphabet: a 23/23 perfect match. CHART is thematically suggestive: Sanborn used “coding charts” to encrypt K4. If confirmed, this would make the three keywords of K4’s stego layer KRYPTOS (alphabet), SEVEN (palette), and CHART (placement). However, ~0.5% of random 5-letter words also produce perfect tables, so CHART is not statistically unique.

Additional tension (March 2025): The Beaufort keystream at crib positions shows 12/24 values from {G,K,O}, an arithmetic progression with step 4 in standard alphabetic indexing (p < 0.001). But under Beaufort KA with key=N, zero source letters map to {G,K,O}. This creates a structural incompatibility: the key=N/SEVEN palette generation mechanism and the GKO crib keystream concentration cannot operate simultaneously. However, the GKO pattern was subsequently found to be unique to standard (AZ) alphabetic indexing and does not appear under KA or other variant/alphabet combinations, suggesting it is an indexing artifact rather than a cipher mechanism signal.

But: This classification was discovered post-hoc (from the data, not predicted in advance). More critically, leave-one-out cross-validation shows the table has zero predictive power: when each of the 17 consensus nulls is held out and predicted from the remaining 16, accuracy is only 47%, worse than the naive 49% baseline of always guessing “null.” Nine of 17 null positions occupy cells unique to them or shared with “real” positions; removing them from training collapses the prediction. The table is a descriptive fit, not a generative rule. It also predicts zero additional nulls among the 14 remaining palette candidates, suggesting the 7 unknown null positions may use non-palette letters or that the 24-null model itself needs revision.

The cipher layer remains open.

What this tells us about K4

What our analysis suggests

  • K4 likely uses two layers: a substitution cipher and a steganographic null-insertion layer (consistent with Sanborn’s public statement about “two systems of enciphering”)
  • The candidate null palette is {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}, which concentrates in two columns of a 5-wide keyword-mixed Polybius grid (specific keyword unknown; KRYPTOS is one of ~6,300 that work)
  • Null placement fits a mod-7 × mod-5 lookup table rule (35/35 correct on training data), but this table is overfit: leave-one-out cross-validation yields 47% accuracy (below the 49% baseline), and it predicts zero additional nulls among candidate positions
  • Null character assignment (which palette letter fills each null slot) is not determined by local arithmetic constraints, position formulas, neighbor functions, keystream echo, or grid cell mapping; all tested models failed. It may require external information such as the encoding chart
  • The Stehle Δ4=5 anomaly (positions 55–63) is a local coincidence: the cipher layer produced consecutive Δ4=5 at non-null pairs, and null insertion extended it because both forced values are palette letters. No global arithmetic rule governs null values (1,248 tests, all non-significant)
  • 17 of 24 candidate null positions are identified by consensus; width-21 bigram optimization narrows the 7 unknowns to a core set of 5 positions ({18,19,56,62,93}), but cipher attacks on all resulting CT73 extractions produce noise
  • The candidate stego and cipher layers appear to share a structural source: the keystream is palette-enriched (13/24, p=0.0043) with joint significance p < 10−7

What's still open

  • The substitution cipher operating on the 73 real characters
  • The key or key-generation method (not periodic, not autokey, not any tested running key, not grid-derived, not null-sequence-derived, not position-dependent)
  • The specific keyword for the Polybius grid (~6,300 candidates remain; KRYPTOS is thematically strongest)
  • The exact identity of 7 uncertain null positions (the mod-35 table cannot predict them; width-21 optimization suggests {18,19,56,62,93} + 2 from {45,46,47,48}, but cipher attacks show no signal on any mask)
  • Whether the true null count is 24 (as assumed from “73”) or 17 (the consensus set); CT80 (17-null extraction) also produces noise under all tested ciphers
  • The physical mechanism (Sanborn ordered a red transparency from Kinko’s, purpose unknown)
