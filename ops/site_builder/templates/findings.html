What the data shows

No single classical cipher decrypts K4. Two statistical anomalies emerged from the analysis. Both were discovered post-hoc (from the data, not predicted in advance), both depend on a specific cipher model (KA-autokey Vigenère) that has not been independently validated, and neither has cracked the cipher. We also document one exploratory pattern that has zero predictive power but is retained for transparency.

How we found these — and what we can’t yet prove

Each observation below is computationally reproducible. However, all were discovered post-hoc, and validation work has revealed important limitations:

  • Independent replication: Richard Bean found the same 5-wide grid structure in 2021 (HistoCrypt 2021) by analyzing different data than we used. Two researchers, different approaches, same result.
  • Shuffled-ciphertext validation: We shuffled K4’s ciphertext 500 times and re-ran the same optimization. Zero shuffled versions produced a palette as restricted as 7 letters (mean: 12.7, min: 9). This indicates the restriction depends on K4’s specific letter ordering, not an artifact of the optimizer (p < 0.002).
  • Model-dependence caveat: The 17 candidate null positions were identified using a KA-autokey Vigenère model. When we re-ran the same process with 4 other cipher models, the positions shifted dramatically (average overlap: Jaccard 0.161). This means the positions are model-conditional, not independently validated. The palette restriction is real under the KA-autokey model but may not be intrinsic to K4 itself.

The Null Palette (RETIRED)

Palette identity failed specificity controls — convergence is generic

Retired
Retired as a finding — controls failed (April 2026)

Status: Retired

Under a specific cipher model (KA-autokey Vigenère), simulated annealing identifies 17 candidate filler positions that use only 7 letters (B, G, I, K, O, W, and Z). The odds of this restriction happening by chance are about 1 in 16,000 under that model.

However, matched controls (April 2026) showed this is not specific to BGIKOWZ. Among 100 random 7-letter palettes, BGIKOWZ ranked near the bottom for cross-model mask agreement (1st percentile on Jaccard-17, 3rd percentile on Jaccard-24). The cross-model convergence improvement from palette constraints is a generic combinatorial property of constraining the search space, not evidence that these particular 7 letters reflect a real steganographic alphabet.

Palette constraints remain useful as a search regularizer (they make mask-inference problems tractable and exhaustively solvable), but the identity of BGIKOWZ as a privileged K4 signal is retired.

Retired palette identity failed matched controls — convergence is generic, not BGIKOWZ-specific
Technical detail

K4 has 97 characters carved into the sculpture, but our analysis suggests that only 73 may be real ciphertext. Under this hypothesis, the other 24 are filler characters (called nulls) inserted to disguise the message’s true length. This is consistent with Ed Scheidt’s public statement that he “masked the English language” in K4 so that frequency analysis would not help solvers.

Using simulated annealing with a KA-autokey Vigenère model, we identified 17 candidate positions. Those 17 characters use only 7 different letters: B, G, I, K, O, W, and Z. Drawing 17 characters at random from K4’s letter distribution and getting only 7 distinct letters has a probability of roughly 1 in 16,000 (permutation test, 10M trials).

Validation (March 2026): We shuffled K4’s ciphertext 500 times and re-ran the same SA process. Zero shuffled versions produced ≤7 distinct letters (mean: 12.7, min: 9), confirming the restriction is specific to K4’s actual letter ordering (p < 0.002). However, when we tested 4 other cipher models, the candidate positions shifted dramatically (average Jaccard overlap: 0.161). The positions are model-conditional, not independently validated.

Post-hoc observation: 4 of the 5 W’s in the ciphertext fall at candidate null positions. This is a consequence of the palette restriction, not independent evidence.

Why this was retired

The original observation was real but not specific. Under KA-autokey, only {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z} appear at the 17 candidate filler positions, and the shuffled-ciphertext test (p < 0.002) confirmed this isn’t an optimizer artifact.

However, four matched controls (April 2026) showed that the cross-model convergence effect is generic:

  • Random palettes: 99 of 100 random 7-letter palettes produced better cross-model agreement than BGIKOWZ.
  • Near neighbors: 76 of 133 single-letter-swap variants beat BGIKOWZ.
  • Shuffled K4: convergence on shuffled ciphertexts was comparable, suggesting the effect is methodological rather than structural.
  • Objective sensitivity: autokey and periodic scoring agreed on 19/24 mask positions (Jaccard 0.655), but the optimum scores differed substantially.
  • Score-conditioned null (April 2026): The actual SA discovery process was run on K4 and 300 null ciphertexts (3 families × 100 each). K4’s SA-discovered consensus used 11 distinct letters, placing it at the 29th percentile of the null distribution (p = 0.30). The original “7 distinct” was traced to a post-hoc selection from positions already containing palette letters — a circular observation. (full report)

The correct interpretation: palette-constrained search is a useful computational technique (it makes mask problems tractable), but the specific palette BGIKOWZ is not a privileged signal from K4’s encryption. Any sufficiently restrictive palette produces similar or better cross-model agreement.

The Stehle Anomaly

A unique constant-difference pattern at positions 55–63

~1 in 642
Moderate confidence

The short version

Every 4th character in one region of K4 (positions 55–63) differs from its predecessor by exactly 5 in the alphabet. This pattern appears nowhere else in the ciphertext. After correcting for 712 statistical tests, the odds of this happening by chance are about 1 in 642.

Technical detail

If you look at every 4th character in K4 starting from position 55, something unusual happens: each character is exactly 5 positions later in the alphabet than the one 4 spots before it. This holds for a run of 9 consecutive characters (positions 55–63).

We searched the entire ciphertext for this kind of pattern at every possible spacing and every possible difference value. This is the only one. After correcting for the 712 statistical tests we ran, the probability of seeing this by chance is about 1 in 642 (Bonferroni-corrected: 712 / 456,976).

What creates this pattern

Two of the 9 positions (58 and 59) are filler characters. The real ciphertext already had this stepping pattern at two consecutive pairs of non-filler characters. The filler values simply extend what was already there, because the required letters (W and I) both happen to be palette letters.

We tested whether this kind of arithmetic forcing is a general rule for how filler values are chosen: it is not. Only 4 of 17 filler positions show this behavior, which is exactly what random placement would produce. We checked all 1,248 possible spacing-and-difference combinations and found no global rule. The Stehle pattern is a local coincidence (a fortunate collision between cipher structure and filler placement), not a mechanism.

KRYPTOS × SEVEN (Exploratory — No Predictive Power)

A post-hoc curve fit with zero cross-validated accuracy

0% predictive (LOO-CV)
Exploratory only — retained for transparency, not evidence

The short version

A post-hoc search found a mod-7 × mod-5 lookup table that perfectly classifies all 35 palette-letter positions as null or real. However, leave-one-out cross-validation shows this table has zero predictive power (47% accuracy vs. 49% baseline). This is a descriptive curve fit, not a discovered rule. We retain it only for transparency — it is not evidence of a mechanism.

Technical detail

We discovered a simple rule that perfectly fits all 35 palette-letter positions, classifying each as either null or real ciphertext. The rule uses two keywords: KRYPTOS (7 letters) and SEVEN (5 letters). This was found by searching the data (post-hoc), not predicted in advance.

For any position p in K4, compute two remainders: divide p by 7 (giving a row in the KRYPTOS keyword) and divide p by 5 (giving a column in the SEVEN keyword). This creates a 7×5 lookup table with 35 cells. Each cell is marked Null or Real. The table correctly predicts all 17 consensus null positions and all 18 real palette positions, with zero errors.

The number 35 is not a coincidence: 7 × 5 = 35, and there are exactly 35 positions in K4 where a palette letter ({B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}) appears. The two keyword lengths produce a repeating cycle of exactly the right size.

Why this is not evidence

Leave-one-out cross-validation shows this table has zero predictive power. When each of the 17 consensus nulls is held out and predicted from the remaining 16, accuracy is only 47% — worse than the naive 49% baseline of always guessing “null.” Nine of 17 null positions occupy cells unique to them or shared with “real” positions; removing any one from training collapses the prediction. The table is a descriptive fit, not a generative rule.

The table also predicts zero additional nulls among the 14 remaining palette candidates, which is inconsistent with the 24-null model it was designed to explain.

Thematic observations (not evidence): KRYPTOS was used for K1–K3. ~0.5% of random 5-letter words produce equivalent tables, so no keyword is statistically unique. The GKO keystream concentration (12/24 crib values from {G,K,O}) was found to be an indexing artifact that disappears under KA alphabet ordering.

Summary

What the analysis produced

  • Palette identity retired (April 2026): The 7-letter palette {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z} was the project’s strongest statistical claim. A score-conditioned null experiment showed that the SA discovery process produces 11 distinct letters on real K4, indistinguishable from shuffled controls (p = 0.30). The original “7 distinct” observation was a post-hoc selection artifact. All downstream claims (BCL enrichment, KA mod-5, AP enrichment) are also retired.
  • A local constant-difference pattern exists at positions 55–63 (the Stehle anomaly, p ≈ 1/642 after correction)
  • The KRYPTOS × SEVEN table has zero predictive power and is not evidence
  • 671 billion+ configurations tested with zero signal above noise across all classical single-layer cipher families

What remains unknown

  • Whether K4 actually contains null characters (the two-system model is plausible but unproven, and the palette evidence for it has been retired)
  • The cipher type and key (every standard single-layer classical cipher has been eliminated under direct correspondence)
  • Whether any null-insertion model is correct — the 17-position consensus and the BGIKOWZ palette were retired as artifacts in April 2026
  • The source text for a running-key cipher, if that model is correct (the only structured non-periodic model surviving Bean constraints)
  • Whether the answer requires external evidence (physical investigation, unreleased documents, K5) rather than further computation

All analysis is open source and independently reproducible. If you see an error, report it.
