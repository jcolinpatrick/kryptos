Three things we did find

No single classical cipher decrypts K4. But the analysis wasn't fruitless. Three notable patterns emerged from the data. The first two were confirmed independently; the third was discovered post-hoc and requires further validation. Together they constrain how K4 was constructed, even though the cipher itself remains unbroken.

Why we trust these findings

Each finding is based on reproducible analysis, not speculation. Findings 1 and 2 were confirmed by independent evidence from multiple directions:

  • Independent replication: Richard Bean found the same 5-wide Polybius structure in 2021 (HistoCrypt) by analyzing the keystream. We found it independently by analyzing the null characters. Two researchers, different data, same result.
  • Exhaustive verification: We tested all 232 million possible placements of the remaining null positions. The 17 consensus positions appear in every configuration that scores above random noise.
  • Cross-layer convergence: The Beaufort keystream at the first 8 BERLINCLOCK positions (63–70) is 7/8 palette letters (p=0.0006). Overall, 13/24 keystream values at all crib positions are palette letters (p=0.0043). This alphabet-independent coupling between the cipher and stego layers means they share a common structural source.
  • Combined odds of coincidence: p < 10−7 (Fisher’s method on palette restriction + keystream enrichment; confirmed by joint Monte Carlo, 0 hits in 10M trials).

The entire codebase is open source. Every finding includes a reproduction command.

1

The Null Palette

Only 7 of 26 letters are used as filler characters

p < 10−7

What we found

K4 has 97 characters carved into the sculpture, but our analysis indicates only 73 are real ciphertext. The other 24 are filler characters (called "nulls") inserted to disguise the message's true length. Ed Scheidt, retired Chairman of the CIA Cryptographic Center who consulted on Kryptos, stated that he “masked the English language” in K4 so that frequency analysis would not help solvers.

We identified 17 positions where researchers agree the character is a null. Those 17 characters use only 7 different letters: B, G, I, K, O, W, and Z. Drawing 17 characters at random from K4’s letter distribution and getting only 7 distinct letters has a probability of roughly 1 in 33,000 (permutation test, 10M trials). This is not coincidence.

Even more striking: 4 of the 5 W's in the entire ciphertext are nulls. The single non-null W sits at position 48, exactly halfway between the two known plaintext regions.

What this means, and what it doesn't

This tells us which letters can appear as filler: only {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}, drawn from two columns of a 5-wide keyword-mixed Polybius grid. It does not tell us which specific palette letter fills each null position. We tested 7 models (position formulas, neighbor functions, keystream echo, grid cell mapping, cipher determination, delta constraints) and all failed (best: 6/17). The character assignment requires information not present in the ciphertext, most likely Sanborn’s encoding chart.

The specific keyword is unknown; KRYPTOS is one of ~6,300 English words that produce this column pattern. It does not crack the cipher operating on the remaining 73 real characters.

The Stehle Anomaly

A unique constant-difference pattern at positions 55–63

p ≈ 0.0016

What we found

If you look at every 4th character in K4 starting from position 55, something unusual happens: each character is exactly 5 positions later in the alphabet than the one 4 spots before it. This holds for a run of 9 consecutive characters (positions 55–63).

We searched the entire ciphertext for this kind of pattern at every possible spacing and every possible difference value. This is the only one. After correcting for the 712 statistical tests we ran, the probability of seeing this by chance is about 1 in 642 (Bonferroni-corrected: 712 / 456,976).

The tension

Two of the 9 positions in this pattern (58 and 59) are consensus null positions. If we remove them, the pattern disappears. This creates a puzzle: either the null mask is wrong at those positions, or the pattern is created by the null insertion process, or it's a rare coincidence at the 0.16% level. No decryption has been extracted from it.

3

KRYPTOS × SEVEN

A dual-keyword lookup table predicts every null position

35 / 35 correct

What we found

We discovered a simple rule that perfectly classifies all 35 palette-letter positions as either null or real ciphertext. The rule uses two keywords: KRYPTOS (7 letters) and SEVEN (5 letters).

For any position p in K4, compute two remainders: divide p by 7 (giving a row in the KRYPTOS keyword) and divide p by 5 (giving a column in the SEVEN keyword). This creates a 7×5 lookup table with 35 cells. Each cell is marked Null or Real. The table correctly predicts all 17 consensus null positions and all 18 real palette positions, with zero errors.

The number 35 is not a coincidence: 7 × 5 = 35, and there are exactly 35 positions in K4 where a palette letter ({B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}) appears. The two keyword lengths produce a repeating cycle of exactly the right size.

Why this matters

The KRYPTOS keyword was used for K1–K3. SEVEN connects to FIVE, which appears at the seam of the Kryptos cylinder. The Beaufort cipher with key N transforms {E,H,N,Q,S,T,V} into the null palette, and that source set contains the word SEVEN. This is an elegant, hand-executable system consistent with 1990 CIA tradecraft.

The N/R cell assignments in the table can be generated by Vigenère(KRYPTOS, CHART) on the standard alphabet: a 23/23 perfect match. CHART is thematically coherent: Sanborn used “coding charts” to encrypt K4. If confirmed, the three keywords of K4’s stego layer would be KRYPTOS (alphabet), SEVEN (palette), and CHART (placement). However, ~0.5% of random 5-letter words also produce perfect tables, so CHART is not statistically unique.

But: This classification was discovered post-hoc (from the data, not predicted in advance), so its statistical significance is hard to calibrate. And it solves how nulls were placed, not how the underlying 73 characters were encrypted. The cipher layer remains open.

What this tells us about K4

What our analysis indicates

  • K4 uses two layers: a substitution cipher and a steganographic null-insertion layer
  • The null palette is {B,G,I,K,O,W,Z}, concentrating in two columns of a 5-wide keyword-mixed Polybius grid (specific keyword unknown; KRYPTOS is one of ~6,300 that work)
  • Null placement follows a mod-7 × mod-5 lookup table rule (35/35 correct)
  • Null character assignment (which palette letter fills each null slot) is not determined by position, neighbors, keystream, or grid cell; it requires external information (likely the encoding chart)
  • The 7×5 table itself can be generated by Vigenère(KRYPTOS, CHART) on the standard alphabet (23/23 perfect match), and CHART is operationally coherent with Sanborn’s documented use of “coding charts”
  • 17 of 24 null positions are confirmed; 6 of the 7 remaining cluster in the table’s “false positive” positions
  • The stego and cipher layers share a structural source: the keystream is palette-enriched (13/24, p=0.0043) with joint significance p < 10−7

What's still open

  • The substitution cipher operating on the 73 real characters
  • The key or key-generation method (not periodic, not autokey, not any tested running key, not grid-derived)
  • The specific keyword for the Polybius grid (~6,300 candidates remain; KRYPTOS is thematically strongest)
  • The exact identity of 7 uncertain null positions (narrowed from 232M to ~6,400 candidates via the false-positive connection, but crib scoring cannot discriminate among them)
  • The physical mechanism (Sanborn ordered a red transparency from Kinko’s, purpose unknown)
